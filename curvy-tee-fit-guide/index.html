Chapter 1

Why Standard Tees Fail Curvy Bodies

Let's say the quiet part out loud: most custom tees were never designed for curvy bodies. They were designed for a straight-size body, then graded up — meaning somebody just made everything bigger without rethinking anything about the shape. Here's what that actually does to your customer:

  • Tiny armholes. The #1 complaint. When a pattern is just scaled up, the armhole doesn't grow the way a real arm does. Result: digging, pinching, and a shirt she rips off the second she gets home.
  • The tent-or-sausage trap. Too small across the bust and it clings and pulls. Size up to fix the bust and now she's swimming in fabric at the waist with sleeves down to her elbows. There is no winning — because the shirt wasn't built for her.
  • Designs placed for somebody else's body. A graphic centered for a medium lands in a completely different spot on a 3X. Text stretches across the fullest part of the bust and becomes unreadable. A cute little left-chest logo gets swallowed whole.
  • Necklines that choke. Standard crew necks sit higher and tighter on a fuller bust and shorter neck, turning a cute tee into a turtleneck she didn't order.
"Fat girls have money too. I would gladly spend tons of money on your site if you carried my size." — A REAL CUSTOMER, ON A THREADLESS FORUM

Here's the beautiful part, though: this is your opening. Big sellers ignore this customer. You won't. A seller who gets fit right doesn't just make a sale — she makes a customer who tells every curvy friend she has. And trust me, we talk.

Chapter 2

Measure Yourself Right

Before you can pick the right size, you need real numbers — not the size you wish you were, not the size you were in 2019. Grab a soft measuring tape (the floppy kind, not the metal toolbox kind) and let's do this in five minutes.

The four numbers that matter

  1. Bust: Wrap the tape around the fullest part of your bust, keeping it level all the way around your back. Wear a good everyday bra — not a sports bra squishing everything down, not nothing at all. The tape should be snug but not tight. You should be able to slip one finger under it.
  2. Waist: Find your natural waist — it's usually the narrowest part, about an inch above your belly button. Stand relaxed (no sucking in, gorgeous — we're getting accurate numbers, not auditioning).
  3. Hips: Around the fullest part of your hips and backside, tape level. This one matters because tees that fit the bust sometimes cling at the hip — and nobody wants a shirt that rides up.
  4. Height: Barefoot, back against a wall. Height tells you whether a "long" body will turn a standard tee into a crop top. If you're 5'9" or taller, length matters as much as width.
Golden rule: write the numbers down and measure every six months. Bodies change, and guessing is how returns happen. If you can, have a friend do the bust — it's nearly impossible to keep the tape level on yourself.
Seller tip: put a little "how to measure" graphic right in your listings. Customers who measure correctly buy correctly — and they don't return.

Chapter 4

Picking the Right Size: The "Ease" Method

Forget everything confusing about junior vs. missy vs. unisex sizing. There's one simple idea that never lies: ease. Ease is just the difference between your body measurement and the shirt's measurement. It's the wiggle room.

Here's how to use it in plain English:

  1. Take your bust number from Chapter 2.
  2. Look at the blank's size chart — find the garment chest measurement (that's the shirt laid flat, measured armpit to armpit, then doubled).
  3. Subtract your bust from the garment chest. That number is your ease.

What your ease number means

  • 2–4 inches of ease → Fitted. Skims close to the body. Pretty, but on a curvy bust it can pull at buttons of text and cling. Only recommend this if she wants fitted.
  • 4–6 inches of ease → Comfortable / true to size. The sweet spot for most curvy customers. The fabric skims instead of clinging, nothing pulls, she can breathe and move.
  • 6–8+ inches of ease → Relaxed / flowy. The cozy oversized look. Great for Comfort Colors wearers and anyone who likes drape.

The curvy rule of thumb: aim for at least 4–6 inches of ease at the bust. Then do a gut-check on the hips too — if her hips are fuller than her bust, make sure the shirt's hip area (roughly the bottom hem width) has room as well, or the shirt will ride up and she'll spend all day tugging it down.

Seller superpower: translate this into one line in your listings — "For a relaxed fit like the model, choose 4–6 inches bigger than your bust measurement." Watch your "what size should I get?" messages melt away.
Chapter 5

Design Sizing & Placement Rules for Curvy Bodies

This is where most sellers blow it without realizing. A design sized and placed for a medium looks lost, stretched, or just plain wrong on a 3X. These rules fix that:

Size the design to the body

The standard chest print most sellers use is about 10–11 inches wide. On a 2X and up, that same print looks like a postage stamp. Go 1–2 inches wider than standard for 2X–3X, and up to 2–3 inches wider for 4X–5X. The design should feel proportional to the canvas — a bigger shirt is a bigger canvas, so use it.

Place it lower than you think

On a curvy body, the neckline sits higher relative to the bust, so a design placed at "standard" height ends up riding on top of the bust instead of below it. Start the design about a hand's width — 3 to 4 inches — below the neckline seam on 2X and up (a little lower for 4X–5X). Center it on the chest, not on the shirt — on fuller busts those aren't the same spot.

Keep text off the fullest point

Words stretched across the apex of the bust warp and become hard to read — and nobody wants their shirt's message distorted. Nudge text-based designs slightly above or below the fullest point, or choose designs where the text sits in the upper chest area with graphics below.

Necklines matter

  • Wider crew necks sit more comfortably on a fuller bust and shorter neck — no choking.
  • Scoop and V-necks elongate the neck and draw the eye vertically. Very flattering, very requested.
  • Avoid tight, high ribbed collars on plus sizes unless the blank is specifically cut with a wider neck.

🚫 What to avoid

  • Tiny left-chest prints on big bodies. A 3-inch pocket print on a 3X gets visually swallowed. Scale pocket prints up a touch for 2X+.
  • Long vertical text down the bust. It follows every curve and becomes unreadable.
  • Designs that end right at the widest point. A graphic whose edge lands exactly at the fullest hip or bust creates a harsh horizontal line. Extend past it or stop well above it.
  • One-size-fits-all gang sheets. If you're printing the same 10.5" design for every size from S to 4X, your plus customers can tell. Make a second, larger version for 2X+ — it's one extra file and it changes everything.
Chapter 6

Fabric, Wash & Care: Keep Prints Alive

Nothing kills a repeat customer faster than a print that dies in the wash. (Remember the buyers whose brand-new tees were "ruined after the first cold wash"? Yeah. Not you.) Print this care card and ship it with every order:

  • Wash inside out. This one step does more than anything else — it keeps the print from rubbing against other clothes and the machine drum.
  • Cold water, gentle cycle. Hot water is the enemy of every print method: DTF, vinyl, screen print, all of them.
  • Skip the high-heat dryer — or at least tumble dry low. High heat cracks prints and shrinks cotton. Hang-dry is the gold standard if she has the patience.
  • Never iron directly on the design. Iron inside-out or lay a cloth over the print.
  • No bleach, no fabric softener on the print. Both break down adhesives and inks over time.

Your secret weapon: wash-test your own shirts and photograph it. "Here's our design after 20 washes" with a real photo beats any promise you could write. One wash-test photo in your listing is worth a thousand words of "high quality print!!!"

Fabric note: 100% cotton (like all three blanks in Chapter 3) can shrink a little on the first hot wash — another reason to preach cold water. Pre-shrunk doesn't mean never-shrinks.
Chapter 7

The Seller's Listing Checklist

You can have the best-fitting tee on the internet and still lose the sale if your listing doesn't prove it. Plus-size buyers have been burned before — they're shopping with their guard up. Here's exactly what to put in every listing to win them over:

  • A real size chart — garment measurements (chest, length), not just "S / M / L." Borrow it from the blank manufacturer's chart and put it in the photos, not buried in the description.
  • Model measurements + the size she's wearing. "Model is 5'6", bust 44", wearing 2X" tells a curvy buyer more than ten adjectives.
  • Photos on curvy bodies. Not one token photo — show the design on 2X and 3X bodies styled well. If you can't hire models yet, AI mockups on plus-size bodies are a legitimate starting point (just keep them honest and un-retouched).
  • Honest fit notes in plain words. "Runs roomy — size down for a fitted look" or "Fashion fit — size up one for a relaxed fit." One line. It builds trust instantly.
  • A wash-test photo. "After 20 washes" with a real picture. This single image answers the fear every buyer has.
  • Fabric content and weight. "100% ring-spun cotton, 6.1 oz" tells an experienced buyer exactly what she's getting.
  • Care instructions in the listing. Inside out, cold wash, low heat. It protects your prints and cuts down on "the design peeled!" complaints.
  • Size-inclusive language. Say "2X–5X" proudly in the title. Never "plus sizes available (upcharge applies)" as an afterthought — and think hard before charging extra for bigger sizes. Nothing says "you're an inconvenience" like a fat tax.
Answer "will this actually fit me?" before she has to ask — and she'll buy from you instead of the shop that made her guess. — YOUR NEW LISTING PHILOSOPHY
